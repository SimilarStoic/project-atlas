## Editorial design

### Topic / central idea

A credit card can be paid in full today while a credit report still reflects an earlier reported balance, so the two
records can temporarily tell different stories.

### Audience value

Young UK adults often assume that paying in full must make every displayed balance and score update immediately. This
explains the lag without shame, reduces needless worry, and gives a useful check before a major credit application.

### Core takeaway

Keep paying on time and in full, understand that credit-file balances update on reporting cycles, and check the report
early rather than treating one score movement as proof that something went wrong.

### Target

- **Duration:** approximately 48–54 seconds
- **Format:** 1080×1920 vertical short-form
- **Audio-only comprehension:** mandatory
- **CTA:** none; the payoff is the practical action

### Evidence and editorial boundaries

The execution Research Pack should freeze current supporting evidence from UK credit-reference sources:

- Experian explains utilisation as used revolving credit divided by available credit, says lenders may consider it,
  and notes that reported balances may take roughly four to eight weeks to update.
- Equifax identifies payment history and utilisation as score factors.
- TransUnion's consumer guidance identifies historic balances and limits as credit-file information.

Do not claim that every lender reports on the statement date, that a particular utilisation percentage guarantees a
score outcome, or that one consumer score equals a lender's decision. Do not encourage carrying interest-bearing debt,
unnecessary early payment, or a higher limit. Payment on time and in full remains sound baseline behavior.

## Exact Production #2 script

> You paid your credit card in full. So why can your credit report still show a balance?
>
> Because your card has two versions of now.
>
> Your banking app shows what you owe today. Your credit report shows the latest balance your lender has reported —
> and that update can take several weeks.
>
> That snapshot helps calculate credit utilisation: the share of your available credit you’re using. A high reported
> balance can affect how lenders view you, even if you’ve since paid it.
>
> The useful move is boring: keep paying on time and in full, check your report before a major application, and if the
> balance looks old, give it time to update.
>
> You didn’t fail at credit cards. One clock just hasn’t caught up with the other.

The script is 126 words. It uses no visual-only fact and makes no guarantee about a score or lending outcome.

## Structure

1. **Hook:** the apparent contradiction between full payment and a displayed balance.
2. **Setup:** introduce “two versions of now.”
3. **Explanation:** live banking balance versus the lender's latest reported snapshot.
4. **Visual analogy:** utilisation as the portion of available space currently shown as occupied.
5. **Practical payoff:** pay reliably, check early, allow stale information to update.
6. **Closing line:** one clock has not caught up with the other.

## Motion and edit plan

Use two conceptual cuts, one reaction punch-in, sequential prop reveals, sparse layer translation, one utilisation
fill/settle action and the closing clock catch-up. Preserve stillness between emphasis points. Avoid uniform Ken Burns
motion, complex character animation, lip sync and generic AI video. Scene duration follows actual narration ideas after
alignment; the table does not impose equal clocks.

## Caption plan

Derive compact cues from actual spoken-word timing. Use mobile-readable type in safe lower/off-white fields, moving the
caption region only when needed to protect the mascot or action. Captions remain subordinate and may omit redundant
on-screen prop labels; no essential meaning depends on them. Never use scene clocks, word counts or fixed words per
second as timing evidence.
